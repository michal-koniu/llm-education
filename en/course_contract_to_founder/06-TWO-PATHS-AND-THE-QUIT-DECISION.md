# Part 06 · Two paths and the quit decision

> **Goal of this part.** You understand the two ways to build a scalable company — **bootstrapped**
> (funded by the contract and by customers) and **venture-funded** — and what each requires from you,
> step by step. You learn how the Polish pre-seed market works in 2025–2026, what investors will check,
> and what "doing a startup" means in practice if you choose it. Then you write the conditions under which
> you will reduce or end the contract — before emotion writes them for you.
>
> **Time:** ~5 h + three investor conversations · **Level:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐ · **Produces:** `founder/06-path.md`,
> `founder/06-quit-triggers.md`

> **Not investment or legal advice.** Market data as of 25 Oct 2026. Fundraising in depth — the pitch deck,
> term sheets, negotiation — is a separate course in this track; this part gives you the map and the
> decision.

---

## 0. The problem

You said you want a company that scales by adding expert agents, and that you could start with or without
venture funding — but if you choose venture, you want to know how. The two paths are not two ways of doing the
same thing. They lead to different companies, with different obligations, and the choice affects the contract
you are now protecting. Making it by default ("everyone raises a pre-seed") or by mood ("I'm sick of the bank") is
the mistake this part prevents.

---

## 1. Is the AI box venture-scale?

Venture funds need a few companies in each fund to return the whole fund. So the first question an investor asks
is not "is this a good business?" but "**can this become very large?**" Do the arithmetic before they do.

Counts from the pricing course (part 03), all 2025 data, with caveats there:

| Buyers in Poland | Count |
| :-- | --: |
| Practising advocates | ~23,600 |
| Legal advisers (on the lists) | 56,415 |
| Tax advisers | 9,220 |
| Notaries (offices) | 3,507 |
| Accounting offices (estimate) | ~70,000 |

These are people and offices, not buyers of a box: many lawyers share one office, many offices will never buy. Take
roughly **100,000 offices** as the upper bound of the Polish professional-office market and test shares of it at the
illustrative *Office* price of PLN 1,790 a month (pricing course, part 07):

| Offices paying | Share of ~100k | ARR (PLN) | ARR (≈ EUR, at 4.25) |
| --: | --: | --: | --: |
| 100 | 0.1% | 2.1 M | 0.5 M |
| 1,000 | 1% | 21.5 M | 5.1 M |
| 5,000 | 5% | 107 M | 25 M |

**Reading it honestly:**

- **100–1,000 offices** is a very good bootstrapped company — a few million złoty to tens of millions of ARR, owned
  by you.
- **Venture scale needs more than Poland**: the same box for professional offices in other EU countries (the repo's
  own profile says *"PL, then EU"*), more professions, or a platform others build agents on. An investor will ask for
  that story on day one. If you do not believe in it, do not raise venture money.
- The **agent catalogue** helps both paths (pricing course, part 08): revenue per office grows without a new sale.

## 2. Path A: bootstrapped

| | |
| :-- | :-- |
| **Money from** | the contract surplus (part 04), then customers: setup fees, annual prepayment, subscriptions |
| **You keep** | 100% of the company, and the freedom to grow at your pace |
| **You give up** | speed; the option to fund rent-model hardware at scale (pricing course, part 05); a team early |
| **The contract** | stays as long as it is useful — it is your investor |
| **Fits when** | the market is Poland-first, you value control, the numbers work at 100–1,000 offices |

Bootstrapping does not close the door to investors. A company with paying customers, measured churn and a growing
catalogue raises later on better terms, if it wants to — or never needs to. Private SaaS companies valued by SaaS
Capital: 4.8× ARR bootstrapped vs 5.3× equity-backed (Jan 2025, *self-reported*). The gap in value is smaller than
the gap in ownership.

You asked to be taught how to do it. Here is the sequence, with what the research found about Poland. Treat the

### 3.3 What investors will require before they invest

| Requirement | Detail | Where in this track |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **A company** | usually a **sp. z o.o.**; a **PSA** (simple joint-stock company) is possible: PLN 1 of capital, shares may be issued **for work or services** — but PSA shares cannot be listed on the GPW or NewConnect | planned legal-form course |
| **IP owned by the company** | a written transfer of everything you built (part 03) — "chain of title" | part 03 |
| **Founders full-time** | YC: founders *"commit to working full-time"*; Antler: *"a full-time commitment"*; investors quoted by LaunchVic call part-time a *"red flag"*. **No explicit Polish statement found** — field experiment 06 asks | §5 below |
| **Founder vesting** | European norm: **4 years with a 1-year cliff**, as *reverse* vesting with good/bad leaver terms; a Polish-law-specific source was not found (in practice often call options in the shareholders' agreement — *general knowledge, unverified*) | planned fundraising course |
| **A team** | YC accepts solo founders but says *"one-person startups are tough"*. For you, the gap is a **domain co-founder** (a lawyer or a tax adviser), as the repo already concluded | planned team course |

### 3.4 The instrument and the price

In the US, **93% of pre-seed rounds in Q2 2026 were SAFEs** (Simple Agreements for Future Equity), 91% of them
post-money; median 2025 post-money caps were about **USD 10 M** for USD 250k–1 M rounds (Carta, US data). YC's
standard deal: USD 125k for 7% on a post-money SAFE plus USD 375k on an uncapped SAFE.

A SAFE is a US-law instrument. Polish deals use Polish-law equivalents (convertible loans and similar agreements) —
**this course's research did not cover them**; the fundraising course will.

The arithmetic you need now is dilution:

```
 post-money valuation = pre-money + investment
 investor's share     = investment / post-money

 example: PLN 2 M invested at PLN 8 M pre-money → post-money PLN 10 M → investor owns 20%, you keep 80%
          two more rounds at 20% each → you keep 80% × 80% × 80% ≈ 51%
```

The example numbers are illustrations, not Polish market data.

### 3.5 The process (general practice)

```
 1. evidence      paid pilots, measured hours, first cohort        (pricing course, parts 06–08)
 2. company       sp. z o.o. / PSA, IP transferred, founders' agreement
 3. list          20–40 funds that invest in your stage and sector (§3.2 is a start)
 4. intros        warm introductions beat cold e-mails — founders they funded, angels, accelerators
 5. deck + model  the problem, the box, the numbers you measured, the EU story, the team, the ask
 6. meetings      partner meeting → second meeting → investment committee
 7. term sheet    valuation, amount, instrument, board, vesting, investor rights
 8. due diligence IP chain of title, contracts, taxes, the founders' other obligations (your bank contract!)
 9. closing       signing, notary (for sp. z o.o.), registration, money
```

Step 8 is where parts 02–04 of this course pay off: the investor's lawyer will read your bank contract, your IP
register and your tax records. **Fundraising is itself close to a full-time job for 3–6 months** — another reason it
does not fit next to a contract.

## 4. Which path, and when to decide

You do not have to decide now, and you should not. Decide at **gate 2** of part 04 (≥ 10 paying offices, measured
churn), when you have the numbers an investor would ask for anyway. Until then, keep both paths open by doing the
things both require: clean IP (part 03), a paid pilot, measured value.

| If at gate 2… | Then lean towards |
| :-- | :-- |
| demand exceeds what you can serve; the EU story is credible; a domain co-founder is ready to join full-time | **venture** |
| growth is steady, margins are good, you can serve demand with one or two helpers | **bootstrapped** |
| growth is slow, churn high | neither — the product needs to change, or the stop condition of part 04 applies |

## 5. The quit decision

### 5.1 Triggers

Write them now. **Any one** of these is a trigger to reduce or end the contract:

| Trigger | How to measure it |
| :-- | :-- |
| **T1 · Funding that requires it** | a term sheet signed with a full-time condition |
| **T2 · The startup pays for your life** | startup gross profit per month ≥ your living costs (part 01). Example: life PLN 12,000 a month; at a 60% gross margin you need PLN 20,000 of monthly subscription revenue — about 11 offices on *Office* at PLN 1,790 |
| **T3 · Time is the bottleneck** | for two consecutive months, deals were lost or delayed because you had no daytime hours (weekly reviews of part 05) |
| **T4 · The contract ends anyway** | notice from the bank, or a change you do not accept |

And the **conditions** that must also hold before you act on T2 or T3 (not on T1 or T4, which force the decision):

- the safety buffer of part 04 is full;
- at least one paying customer has renewed (the top of the evidence ladder in the pricing course);
- the people at home agree.

### 5.2 Not all-or-nothing

A B2B contract can often be reduced before it is ended. Options to negotiate, in order:

1. **Fewer days a week** — e.g. three days for the bank, two for the startup.
2. **A fixed project instead of a daily rate** — results and deadlines, no availability window; this also moves the
   contract away from employment indicators (part 02, §6.3).
3. **Ending with the full notice period**, offering a handover. Banks remember professional exits; your reputation in
   IT is part of your future hiring and partner network.

### 5.3 The decision memo

When a trigger fires, write one page before acting: the trigger, the evidence, the conditions, the option you choose
from §5.2, and what would make you reverse it. The same discipline as an ADR with a falsification condition in the
technical courses.

## 7. ⭐ FIELD EXPERIMENT 06 — ask the investors

**Hypothesis.** Polish pre-seed investors will invest in a founder who still holds a contract **before** the round,
but will require full-time commitment **from** the round, and will see a solo technical founder without a domain
co-founder as the main weakness of the AI box.

**Procedure.** Ask for 20-minute conversations with three investors at your stage (§3.2 — at least one PFR
Starter-backed fund). Say openly that you are **not raising now** and want advice. Ask:

1. *"Would you invest in a founder who keeps a B2B contract until the round? And after?"*
2. *"What would you need to see from an on-premise AI box for professional offices before a pre-seed?"*
3. *"How do you look at hardware in a subscription business?"*
4. *"What is the biggest weakness of this idea, as I described it?"*

**Prediction (mine).** All three: contract fine before, full-time required after. Two of three name the missing domain
co-founder; at least one asks for the EU expansion story; at least one is sceptical of hardware and asks about leasing.

## 8. Pitfalls

**B26 · Raising before evidence.**
*Symptom:* a pitch deck before a single paid pilot. *Why it hurts:* a low valuation or a "no" that is hard to reverse.
*Remedy:* evidence first (pricing course, parts 06–08); conversations without an ask (field experiment 06).

**B27 · Quitting on a good week.**
*Symptom:* two great meetings, and you give notice. *Remedy:* the triggers and conditions of §5, measured over months.

**B28 · Venture money without a venture story.**
*Symptom:* raising for a Poland-only business that tops out at a few hundred offices. *Remedy:* the arithmetic of §1;
bootstrap if you do not believe in the larger story.

**B29 · The solo technical founder.**
*Symptom:* no lawyer or tax adviser deeply involved in the product. *Remedy:* look for the domain partner now, starting
with the people from your pricing interviews.

**B30 · Forgetting that due diligence reads your past.**
*Symptom:* a term sheet, then the investor's lawyer finds the broad IP clause in the bank contract. *Remedy:* parts 02–03
done long before fundraising.
